Research On-Ramp

Think you may have a Mayflower connection? Begin where you stand, build backward one generation at a time, and let evidence guide the journey.

Many people begin with a treasured family tradition, a familiar surname, or an online tree. That spark of curiosity is where the journey starts, but proving a lineage requires stepping aboard a methodical path of documented parent-to-child links.

Orientation & Guidance Notice

This page provides educational guidance for researchers exploring a potential Mayflower connection. It does not determine membership eligibility and is not an application for membership. Official lineage verification requires direct documentary evidence for every generation, formally reviewed through the a Society Historian and certified by the General Society in Plymouth.

1. Begin Where You Stand

The single most common mistake in Mayflower genealogy is starting at 1620 and trying to work forward. With hundreds of thousands of living descendants, working downward quickly turns into an unmanageable thicket.

Instead, work strictly from the present backward. Document yourself first, then your parents, your grandparents, and earlier ancestors generation by generation. Never jump over a missing generation or assume a link based solely on a matching surname.

1

Identify

Full names, birth dates, death dates, and marriage dates for each ancestor.

2

Locate

Exact towns and counties where vital events took place.

3

Primary vital records, probate files, land deeds, and church registers.

4

Explicit evidence connecting child to biological parents for each step.

2. Distinguish Clues from Proof

Genealogical clues are valuable because they suggest where to look next, but clues alone cannot establish membership. Understanding the difference between a research lead and primary proof saves hours of frustration.

Useful Clues (Starting Points) Acceptable Proof (Documentation)
A family story or tradition passed down orally Certified birth, marriage, and death records naming parents
Autosomal DNA matches, shared cM estimates, or genetic cousin clusters Contemporaneous documentary paper trail proving direct parent-to-child biological descent
Unverified trees on Ancestry, FamilySearch, MyHeritage, 23andMe, or WikiTree, etc. Contemporaneous wills, probate distributions, and land deeds
A shared surname (e.g., Bradford, Brewster, Cooke, Warren) Church baptisms, marriage registers, and town clerk record books
Undocumented family Bibles or unsourced county histories Federal/state census records paired with direct parentage documentation

3. Bridging the Middle Generations

For most modern applicants, proving the line down from 1620 is already handled for the first five generations through the published scholarship of the General Society (known as the Silver Books). Proving your recent three or four generations is straightforward using modern civil vital records.

The real challenge of Mayflower research almost always lies in the middle generations (Generations 5 through 9), spanning the late 1700s and 1800s as families migrated westward. This is where you will focus your search on wills, deeds, tax records, and court documents to connect your 19th-century ancestors back to known colonial lines.
